The evidence

Confirmed

Exhibit ATwo factories, one state

Most of the glitter on America’s name-brand products comes from two factories. Both are in New Jersey. Glitterex is in Cranford, founded 1963. Meadowbrook Inventions is in Bernardsville, and it’s the original.

The reporter wasn’t allowed to see glitter being made. She wasn’t even allowed to hear it being made. Out of all Glitterex’s clients, exactly one agreed to be named: Revlon.

You can’t even LISTEN to it??

The boring truth

Factories guard their machines and client lists all the time. A business lecturer on the podcast Endless Thread had a name for it: “It’s called good business.”

Confirmed

Exhibit BA farmer’s trash machine

1934. Henry Ruschmann, a New Jersey machinist who kept Guernsey cows, builds a high-speed machine that cuts apart rolls of people’s glossy photo prints. When it stutters, it sprays shiny scraps across the floor. His workers take them home as snow for their Christmas trees. He calls them schnibbles.

In World War II, glass glitter dries up, so he starts grinding up scrap plastic instead. By 1948 he’s making 80 pounds of glitter a day on the farm. The glitter pays for the cows.

Trash in. Sparkle out. Cows funded.

The boring truth

This is just glitter’s origin story, and it’s well documented. He filed four patents on cutting film and foil, and his company now sells over 20,000 kinds of glitter. People have loved shiny stuff for a long time: Egyptians put ground malachite in make-up, and the Maya mixed mica into temple paint.

Half true

Exhibit CThe man who made glitter helped make the bomb

In 2023 a YouTuber called Chuppl tracked down Ruschmann’s son. His story: in World War II, the US government hired his father to cut mica washers for the atomic bomb. An old New Jersey newspaper clipping says he worked on the Manhattan Project too.

Chuppl’s video came out on 30 June 2023. Three weeks later, Barbie and Oppenheimer opened in cinemas on the same day.

Barbenheimer was a confession.

The boring truth

Glitter came first. It dates to 1934, and the Manhattan Project started in 1942. He got the job because he was good at precision cutting, not because the bomb needed glitter. The only evidence is his son’s word and one clipping. Even the video’s makers said it had nothing to do with how glitter was created.

Not it

Exhibit DThe military already throws shiny stuff at radar

In World War II, the British dropped clouds of aluminium-coated paper strips to fill German radar screens with fake echoes. It’s called chaff, and fighter jets still carry it. The US Army even considered shooting glitter out of the back of planes.

Military-grade confetti.

The boring truth

Chaff isn’t craft glitter, and the Army never went ahead with the glitter idea. A defence aviation analyst told Endless Thread that radar-absorbing materials are built to very exact specs. Glitter would make a plane easier to spot, not harder.

Unexplained

Exhibit EA barn in Germany and a machine called The Devil

Around 1999, Joe Coburn’s father found two old glitter machines in a tiny German village. Buyers offered him €120,000 to €140,000 for one, nearly sight unseen. He only let them look through a barn window. They wanted to copy it.

His father compared glitter’s profit margins to cocaine: you sell it by the gram and customers buy it by the tonne. Today the family runs 22 machines, and one has a name. The Devil. It cuts one material for one customer. What material? Reveal: Nobody will say. Not even to the podcast.

A SECOND secret customer!!

The boring truth

Products made for one client are confidential in every industry. And the cocaine margins are gone: prices have fallen to a third or a quarter of what they were, as competition grew in China and Taiwan.

Coincidence?

Exhibit FThe same week

Friday 21 December 2018: the New York Times asks who buys the glitter. That Monday, a former NASA engineer posted a video of a fake parcel that blasts thieves with a pound of glitter and fart spray. It racked up over 42 million views in four days.

Everybody was talking about glitter. Nobody was asking who buys it.

Distraction? DISTRACTION.

The boring truth

Mark Rober was getting back at the people who stole his parcels. It has nothing to do with the mystery, except that both stories went viral at once. That’s why people still mix them up. As far as anyone can find, neither he nor Veritasium has made a video about the mystery itself.

Confirmed

Exhibit GThey’re feeding it to wombats

Zoos feed animals glitter so they can tell whose poo is whose. At Assiniboine Park Zoo in Canada, every polar bear gets its own colour. The Smithsonian’s zoo has done it with red pandas and cheetahs.

And here in Australia, researchers fed glitter to southern hairy-nosed wombats in golden syrup on Weet-Bix. It came out the other end in about three days.

WEET-BIX. They put it in WEET-BIX.

The boring truth

This is real science. Zoos test droppings for hormones to check on an animal’s health, and the glitter passes straight through. A few grams per animal won’t make anyone the world’s biggest buyer.

Confirmed

Exhibit HThe FBI has a glitter library

There are tens of thousands of different glitters, and they cling to people without anyone noticing. That makes glitter almost perfect trace evidence. FBI Laboratory chemists have matched glitter at a crime scene to glitter in a suspect’s car.

Forensic scientist Edwin Jones keeps one of the biggest glitter collections in the world: more than 1,000 samples.

They’re CATALOGUING it.

The boring truth

Forensic scientists collect glitter to solve crimes, not to buy it by the drum. Their pro tip: the sticky strip on a sticky note picks glitter up better than tape.

Debunked

Exhibit IThe great glitter shortage of 2022

October 2022. A TikTok acting out the reporter’s phone call gets about 6.4 million plays in ten days. Suddenly everyone “knows” there’s a glitter shortage, and that someone is hoarding it.

WHO IS HOARDING IT

The boring truth

Nobody. The Deseret News looked into it and found no credible source for a glitter shortage.

Confirmed

Exhibit JA ban with a paint-shaped loophole

17 October 2023. The European Union bans loose plastic glitter in things like craft kits and toys. Make-up gets until 2035.

But glitter that’s locked inside paint, glue or ink? Not affected. So which industry seals its glitter inside a hard, shiny coat?

Look at the hulls.

The boring truth

The ban targets glitter that can escape into the environment, and glitter sealed in a coating mostly can’t. Australia has no ban. In December 2023, a University of the Sunshine Coast microbiologist called glitter “microplastics in disguise” and asked Australia to review it.

Probably it

Exhibit KThe boats

In 2019 the Endless Thread podcast got a tip from inside the paint industry. A Glitterex customer’s colleague asked at a supplier meeting, and was told: boat manufacturers.

A gel coat chemist backed it up. In the 90s he worked at a bass boat factory that went through about ten 30-gallon drums of glitter a week. His company buys Glitterex glitter through a distributor near Memphis. And Glitterex’s own company blurb lists gel coat as the first industry it serves. Before paint. Before make-up. Before crafts.

Boat parts shops sell it by brand. You can buy 8-ounce bags of “glitter flake” under Tracker Boats and MasterCraft part numbers, for touching up a hull. A fibreglass supplier sells “Glitterflake” for repairing bass boats: up to 20% of the gel coat, by weight.

In the original 2018 article, the manager also said “it” doesn’t look like glitter. Then she changed the subject to automotive-grade pigments. The reporter’s own list of things secretly full of glitter included a jet ski paint job.

Here’s the bit that gets me. Bass fishermen don’t call it glitter. They call it metalflake. On a boating forum, one owner sanding back his old hull realised he actually had glitter, not metalflake, and asked if that was even possible. They don’t want anyone to know it’s glitter.

IT’S THE BOATS

The boring truth

This is the best answer anyone has, but it’s secondhand. Glitterex declined to comment and has never confirmed it. The runner-up, car paint, fell over when a car paint colour lab said it uses “effect pigments” and didn’t know of ever buying from Glitterex.
